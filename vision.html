Choices

  • Human life is a sequence of decisions, and language models are now the most available help with them. But they hold a person as a few unknown weights — too impoverished an account of anyone. We are building a model of a person rich enough to train against, and using it to evaluate and build aids that actually help someone choose.
    • A language model that can reason about a person's choices and preferences
      • Show that frontier models are poor at helping a person reason about their own choices, with a benchmark that measures it
      • Show that they are poor at helping a person choose at all
      • Train a model that reasons about psychology and preferences
        • Two readings of the same thing: a reasoning capability, or a bicycle for choosing
      • Show with real users that our model helps them choose better
    • What we believe, such that the project works if it is true
      • A person is better off choosing with an aid than without one
      • People make mistakes when they choose
      • People cannot easily say what they want — the articulation problem, and the inversion problem behind it
      • There are structured ways to predict a person
      • If this does not work out, one of these was wrong
    • There are no verifiers in the behavioral space
      • Reward environments carried math and code; nothing like them exists for helping a person choose
      • A new way to modularize the human parts of a problem
      • Take people seriously
        • Heterogeneity between people
        • Complexity within one person
      • A shift in how decision aids are judged: not one fixed benchmark, but how an aid performs across the space of users
      • A shift in how they are built: an off-the-shelf model converses directly with the vocalizer
    • The pieces we will produce
      • A neural model of a user
        • Modular, stable on some dimensions and context-dependent on others
        • Carries known effects — a sunk cost, a standing aversion to price — alongside genuine ambivalence
      • A vocalizer that says in English what tendencies the model is exhibiting
        • A blocker, not an information haver: it does not solve the decision, it stands between the aid and the truth
        • It may struggle to pass the network through, and that struggle is itself something to reason about
      • An inversion that recovers an estimated utility function from the vocalizer
      • A reward function, to improve and build and compare decision aids
      • An evaluation over kinds of users rather than over questions
    • What makes it hard
      • People are complex and the area is fuzzy
      • What is known about a person is scattered and not in a computable form
      • Reasoning models need something that scores a trace; it is unclear what scores a trace about a person
      • Structure buys a vocalizer we can trust and costs the flexibility that made the model worth having — the balance is the central problem
      • There will always be dimensions of a person the model misses; we need a reason to call it good enough
      • We have to earn the claim that the model is human-like
    • How it could fail
      • The closer the vocalizer is fit to our own user model, the narrower the contribution
      • The more a domain is taken seriously, the less the model generalizes out of it
      • Learning a person means fighting two problems at once: clarifying what they want, and learning the network
      • Downstream error is hard to attribute between the model and the vocalizer
      • Adding one more aspect of a person turns out not to be modular
    • The order of the work
      • Can start now: pick a first setting, and gather the aids to measure against
      • Once the setting is fixed: build the neural user model
      • Once the model exists: the inverter, the vocalizer, the reward function
      • Once the reward function exists: benchmark the aids, then train our own
      • Separately, and on its own clock: choose the domain that carries the result
    • What to resolve first
      • How each piece is actually built
      • Which dimensions of a person pay for themselves
      • Which setting makes the result undeniable
    • Where the wow is
      • Behavior stops being too fuzzy to touch and becomes something we make technical progress on
      • A step change rather than one more structural model that captures one effect
      • Still open: how we prove it, and where
